# Ultra study rubric (v2, locked 04/11/2026 after the pilot)

v1 was locked before any script was scored. In the pilot (28 scripts, three judges) the judges agreed on hook, format and naturalness
(α 0.74–0.76) but not on substance, voice, ending and accuracy (α −0.09–0.52), always by one point between 4 and 5: v1 described 1, 3 and 5
but not 4, and the same flaw was taken off in different criteria by different judges. v2 adds the 4 of every criterion and the scoring
discipline below; the criteria, weights and 1/3/5 anchors are unchanged.

## Scoring discipline (read before the criteria)
1. **Each flaw counts once, in its home criterion.** AI tics and written-sounding phrasing → Naturalness only. A list where the genre isn't
   a list, a missing format part → Format only. Words over or under the range → Length only. A confident generalisation that is not a
   factual claim ("quase ninguém lembra do colo", "the problem is rarely the phone") → nowhere (it is style); an overreaching factual claim
   (a mechanism, a rule, a health or money effect) → Accuracy. A thin or restating paragraph, an obvious point → Substance. A creator rule,
   the catchphrase, the narrator, the angle, the opening line, literal words, the register → Voice. How the script ends → Ending.
2. **5 is not "no flaw anywhere"; it is "no flaw in this criterion".** A script can have 5 in Substance and 3 in Naturalness.
3. **4 means one minor flaw of the kind the criterion lists**, which the creator would fix in seconds or might leave. Two minor flaws, or one
   that changes how the script lands, is 3.
4. **A call to action the creator's own profile prefers** (their usual ending, in the delivery notes) is part of their voice, not a habit:
   it costs nothing if it follows from the script, and costs one point in Ending only when it is stacked after a payoff that had already
   closed the video.

You judge spoken short-video scripts that an AI wrote for a creator, who will read them on a teleprompter. Judge each script as the
creator would on the day they record: would they film it, film it after a few light edits, or ask again? The scripts are blind: you never
know which model or setting wrote them, and you must not guess. Length alone is never quality: a longer script is better only when the
extra words carry substance the shorter one lacks.

Read the context first (the creator, what they asked, the platform, the format, the length the app asked for). Then read every script once
fully before scoring any, then score each against the anchors below, quoting the words that justify each score below 5.

## Criteria (1–5 each; the weights make the 0–100 total)

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---|
| Hook | 15 |
| Substance | 20 |
| Format and genre | 15 |
| Spoken naturalness | 15 |
| Creator's voice and inputs | 15 |
| Accuracy | 10 |
| Ending | 5 |
| Length | 5 |

Total = Σ weight × (score − 1) / 4. A script with every criterion at 3 scores 50; all at 4 scores 75; all at 5 scores 100.

### Hook (15)
- **5** The first one or two sentences are specific to this idea and open a gap the viewer wants closed (a stake, a surprise, a concrete
  promise, a moment of tension), in the creator's way of speaking. When the request gave an opening line, it is used as given or adapted
  only to the voice, and nothing comes before it.
- **4** Specific and on promise, but the gap is familiar (a pain question every video on the topic opens with, a stock reframe like
  "most X don't fail because of Y").
- **3** On topic and clear, but generic ("Today I'll talk about…", a question anyone could ask), or the gap is weak; or the given opening line
  is used but what follows deflates it.
- **1** A greeting or filler before the point, a promise the script never pays, or a given opening line ignored or buried.

### Substance (20)
- **5** Delivers what the idea and the hook promised, with reasons, a concrete example or scene, and the consequence. Every paragraph adds
  something new; the viewer leaves knowing or feeling something specific. Development fits the length: tight when short, rich when long.
- **4** Delivers the promise completely, but every point is the one a viewer would expect, or one paragraph restates or thins out.
  **5 needs** at least one specific, non-obvious detail, check or example the viewer couldn't have guessed, and no restating paragraph.
- **3** Delivers the promise but thinly: claims without the why, an example that is generic, a paragraph that restates the previous one, or
  some padding.
- **1** Summary, slogans or advice anyone could give; repeats itself; the promise is not delivered.

### Format and genre (15)
Check the format's checklist (below). On Auto, judge whether the genre fits the idea (a story told as a story, an opinion argued, a myth
debunked, a day shown as a day) and whether a list appears only when the idea is a list.
- **5** The format's shape is all there and feels natural, not like a template.
- **4** All parts present, one of them perfunctory (a turn that is only "days later…", a lesson longer than a line or two, a short
  enumerated prescription at the end of an opinion, a myth video that states why the myth seems true in half a sentence).
- **3** Recognisably the format but a key part is missing or weak (a story with no turn, an opinion that drifts into tips, a tutorial with no
  way to check the result, a myth video that never says why the myth seems true); or on Auto a fair genre but not the strongest one.
- **1** The format is ignored, or on Auto the idea is forced into the wrong genre (a story or an opinion turned into "first… second…
  third…").

### Spoken naturalness (15)
Read it aloud in your head at a natural pace.
- **5** Sounds spoken by a real person in this language and register: varied sentence rhythm, everyday words, oral connectors used with
  measure, no stumbles. No AI tics (list below).
- **4** One AI tic or one written-sounding passage (a formal imperative in a casual voice, a recap line, a stacked clause).
- **3** Readable aloud but written-sounding in places (long or stacked clauses, formal connectors, uniform rhythm), or one or two AI tics.
- **1** Essay prose or a chain of AI tics; hard to say aloud.

### Creator's voice and inputs (15)
Use the context: who they are, their audience, how they speak, their rules, their catchphrase, "I" or "we", and every input of the request
(angle, opening line, words to say literally, brand facts, topic).
- **5** Every rule and input respected; sounds like this creator talking to this audience (level, vocabulary, energy, humour as given); the
  catchphrase, if any, at most once and where it fits; the narrator right ("we" for a team); the angle followed through.
- **4** Every rule and input respected, but one thing is slightly off: the register a notch more formal or flatter than the delivery
  notes, the angle followed but one half of it thin, the opening line kept but loosened.
- **3** Mostly right but generic (could be any creator), or one input weakened (angle half-followed, a literal phrase paraphrased, the
  catchphrase forced, register slightly off).
- **1** Breaks a creator rule or a literal requirement, changes the narrator, ignores the angle, or speaks to the wrong audience.
- With the voice off (no creator section in the context), judge only the request's inputs and a neutral, natural voice.

### Accuracy (10)
Imagined everyday scenes, small stories and first-person opinions are allowed (the creator reviews the draft). Not allowed: invented
numbers, measurements, prices, studies, statistics, credentials, endorsements or test results; false mechanisms; universal promises where
the truth depends on conditions; risky advice on health, money or law.
- **5** Nothing invented beyond imagined scenes; conditions said as conditions; nothing risky.
- **4** One claim stated more firmly than it should be, which would not mislead (a guideline said as a rule, a simplified but
  harmless mechanism). Confident generalisations that are style, not facts, are not counted here.
- **3** One soft overreach (a confident generalisation, a vague "experts say", a simplified mechanism that is not wrong enough to mislead).
- **1** An invented number, study or credential, a false claim, or advice that could harm.

### Ending (5)
- **5** Closes what the script built (the payoff, the takeaway, the position) at the right moment; a call to action only when asked or when
  the format needs one (an ad, a launch).
- **4** Closes the script, but the last line is generic (could close any video on the topic), or a recap restates what was said.
- **3** Ends acceptably but flat, abruptly, or with a habitual tagline or question the script didn't build.
- **1** Stops mid-thought, or ends with a reflexive "comment / follow / save" the request never asked for.

### Length (5)
Use the words the app asked for (given in the context) and the measured word count given with each script.
- **5** Inside the asked range (or, when the length is free, proportionate to the subject).
- **4** Up to 3% outside the range (a few words).
- **3** Within 10% outside the range.
- **1** More than 10% outside it.

## Practical verdict (one per script)
- **Record as is**: the creator could film it now.
- **Light edits**: they would change at most about three sentences.
- **Redo**: they would ask again or rewrite a large part.

## Hard flags (tick every one that applies)
`invented_fact` · `broken_text` (missing or doubled word, lost accents, grammar error) · `wrong_language_or_address` ·
`creator_rule_broken` · `literal_missing` · `risky_advice` · `format_ignored` · `wrong_narrator` · `cta_by_habit`

## Format checklists
- **Storytime**: opens in the tension or teases the outcome; the stake is clear early; beats linked by cause (but / so), not "and then"; a
  turn; the payoff answers the opening; any lesson comes out of the story briefly.
- **Tutorial**: the result first; distinct steps in order, doable with what the creator has; how to tell each worked; the common mistake.
- **List / tips**: items that don't overlap, each with its why or how and an example; strongest first or last; transitions that carry meaning.
- **Myth vs fact**: Fact – Myth (flagged) – why it seems true and why it misleads – Fact again with what to do (Debunking Handbook 2020).
- **Opinion / hot take**: the position early; reasons and an example, not a list of tips; the strongest objection answered; closes on the
  position.
- **Review**: first impression, real pros and cons, verdict and who it is for.
- **Sponsored ad**: hook – problem – product – proof – offer and link/code; disclosure; brand facts literal; nothing promised beyond them.
- **POV**: "POV:" situation, second person, present tense, one scene, a turn.
- **Launch**: the news first; what, when, where; why it matters; what to do now.
- **Apology**: what happened and what the apology is for; responsibility without excuses; sorry; what changes and the repair; no hype.
- **Talking head**: one point, why it matters, one example.
- **Auto, by the idea's kind**: story → told as a story; opinion → argued; myth → debunked; fact → explained with its why; before/after → the
  contrast shown; day in the life → the day shown; prediction → argued with reasons; lesson → the story of learning it; list → a list.

## AI tics to notice (count against naturalness, and against voice when they replace the creator's way of speaking)
- "Not X, it's Y" / "não é X, é Y" / "no es X, es Y" used as a device (once may be fine; twice is a tic).
- Everything in threes; stacked triads of adjectives or clauses.
- Rhetorical questions answered at once ("O resultado? …", "The secret? …").
- One-word or fragment punchlines in series ("Simples. Rápido. Barato.").
- Stock phrases: "a verdade é que", "o segredo é", "muda tudo", "virar o jogo", "no final das contas", "pensa comigo", "here's the thing",
  "game changer", "let that sink in", "la verdad es que".
- Inspirational wrap-ups that could close any video; moralising.
- Over-signposting ("Primeiro… Segundo… Terceiro…", "Vamos lá:") where the genre isn't a list.
- Em dashes (the app strips them, but a dash-shaped rhythm can remain).
- Hedged generalities ("muitas pessoas", "especialistas dizem") instead of a concrete case.
